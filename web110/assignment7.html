For Activity 1 I completed the tutorials, completing the tutorials on Internet reference models, Internet protocols, and the Domain Name System (DNS), which helped me understand how devices communicate over a network. The OSI and TCP/IP models explain how data travels between devices, while protocols such as TCP, IP, HTTP, and DNS support communication and access to Internet resources. I also learned how IP addresses, subnet masks, default gateways, and DNS servers work together to provide network connectivity. I applied this knowledge by reviewing my computer’s network settings and learning how to use commands such as ipconfig on Windows or ifconfig on Linux and macOS systems. Using ping, tracert or traceroute, and nslookup demonstrated how to test connectivity, identify routing problems, and verify domain name resolution. These skills will help me troubleshoot common Internet problems at home or in a workplace. For Activity 2, I reviewed my network's IP address settings, which showed me how to verify physical connections, inspect network settings, and communicate with other devices on a local network. Checking Ethernet connection lights or wireless settings is an important first step when a device cannot connect. Testing the local IP address, default gateway, and an external address such as 8.8.8.8 helps narrow down potential problems. I also learned that a successful ping to an IP address does not always guarantee that DNS or every Internet service is working correctly. The file-sharing activities introduced me to Windows shared folders and UNC paths, macOS file-sharing settings, and Samba on Linux. These tools let computers on the same network share documents and other resources. I can apply these skills to share files between my personal computers, transfer school assignments, and collaborate with others while managing permissions to protect private information. For Activity 3, I went over operating systems, licensing, and support. Researching Windows, macOS, and Linux helped me compare their licensing arrangements, support options, and costs. Windows commonly requires a paid license for a new installation, although the price depends on the edition and whether a license is included with the computer. macOS is generally provided without a separate operating-system purchase for compatible Apple computers, while hardware and repair costs can be high. Linux distributions are often available at no charge, although commercial support, enterprise subscriptions, and specialized services may have fees. This comparison showed me that the total cost of an operating system includes more than its initial price. I would also consider security updates, compatibility, ease of use, technical support, and the applications required for school or work before choosing an operating system.
